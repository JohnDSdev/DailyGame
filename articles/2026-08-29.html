Water can darken sand without changing its pigment

A wet patch of pavement or beach sand can look several shades darker even though the water is clear. The change happens because filling the spaces between grains changes how light scatters, how long it stays inside the material, and how likely it is to be absorbed before returning to your eye.

Dry sand is full of boundaries. Light meets air, then a mineral grain, then air again. At each boundary the refractive index changes sharply. Some light turns back toward the surface after only a few encounters, which is part of why a rough dry material can look pale and diffuse.

Put water into those gaps and the boundaries become optically less abrupt. Water's refractive index is closer to that of many transparent mineral grains than air's is. In a 1986 Applied Optics paper, Sean Twomey, Craig Bohren and J. L. Mergenthaler argued that this reduced contrast makes scattering more forward-directed. A photon then tends to travel through more of the particulate material before it finds its way back out. More travel means more chances to be absorbed.

The normal-incidence Fresnel reflectance is R = ((ngrain − nfluid)/(ngrain + nfluid))². The falling value shows the shrinking index contrast; it is not the total reflectance of the sand bed.

That explanation is not the whole story. John Lekner and Michael Dorf revisited the problem in 1988. They extended an older model in which diffuse light inside a wet rough surface can hit the water-air boundary at angles that send it back into the material by total internal reflection. That gives the light another trip through an absorbing surface instead of an immediate escape.

Both mechanisms point in the same visual direction: less light comes back out toward you.

The simple theories do not completely agree with real grains

Sand is annoyingly specific. Grain transparency matters. So does grain roughness, pore geometry, wavelength and the angle from which you illuminate and view the surface. In 2006, Hao Zhang and Kenneth Voss measured dry, wet and submerged particulate layers and concluded that neither the 1986 nor the 1988 theory was sufficient by itself. Their glass-sediment mixtures showed that the fraction of translucent grains can change how strongly wetting darkens a layer.

This is a useful correction because the familiar one-line explanation, "water traps the light," is too tidy. There are several routes by which wetting changes the photon's odds. Which route matters most depends on the material.

Beach sand gives you a spectrum, not one brightness number

A 2014 study by Marjolein van der Wal and colleagues measured beach-sand reflectance from 350 to 2500 nanometres while changing moisture content under laboratory conditions. Reflectance fell nonlinearly as the sand became wetter. In the visible range, the general spectral shape changed much less than the overall brightness, which matches the ordinary impression that wet sand is mostly a darker version of the same color.

Farther into the infrared, water stops being such a quiet participant. The measurements show strong absorption features around 1470 and 1940 nanometres. That matters to remote sensing: a camera outside the visible band can use the spectral change to estimate surface moisture, but it also has to distinguish moisture from grain composition and geometry.

The wet patch is therefore doing something more interesting than wearing a transparent coat. The coat changes the optical network between grains. A lot of the light that used to escape after a short trip now takes the long way around.
