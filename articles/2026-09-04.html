Peeling tape can make X-rays in a vacuum

A strip of ordinary pressure-sensitive tape can turn the mechanical work of peeling into nanosecond X-ray flashes. The vacuum chamber is the part that makes the trick strange rather than merely sparkly.

In 2008, Carlos Camara, Juan Escobar, Jonathan Hird and Seth Putterman put ordinary adhesive tape in a vacuum chamber and peeled it from the roll at about 3 centimetres per second. Their detector saw bursts of X-rays synchronized with the tape's stick-slip motion. The pulses were only nanoseconds long. The spectrum had a peak near 15 keV.

The team went one step further. Nature's cover description for the paper records that they used the peeling tape as the source for an X-ray photograph of a human finger. The apparatus was not a medical machine in miniature. It was a motor, a roll of tape, a vacuum chamber and a detector arranged around the peel point.

At ordinary room pressure, this is not what happens when you pull tape from a dispenser. Air changes the discharge. The X-ray result was observed in vacuum, where separated charge can drive electrons across the gap without the same constant collisions with gas molecules.

The peel line behaves like a tiny electrical machine

Peeling separates two surfaces that had been in contact. That separation can leave opposite charges on the tape and the roll. In the UCLA experiment the electric field across the growing gap accelerated electrons. When those electrons hit matter and slow abruptly, some of their energy can leave as bremsstrahlung: braking radiation. At sufficiently high electron energies, that radiation reaches the X-ray band.

The surprising part was not that friction and fracture can make light. Triboluminescence has a long experimental history, and adhesive tape had already been studied as a source of visible emission. The 2008 paper was striking because the energy was concentrated into brief X-ray pulses strong enough for radiography.

The flashes were tied to stick-slip peeling rather than a perfectly smooth separation. Tape does not always detach at a constant microscopic speed even when the roll turns steadily. The peel front can hold, release, hold again. Camara and colleagues found the X-ray bursts correlated with those release events.

There is a lot packed into a tiny gap

The paper's authors reported a 15 keV spectral peak and discussed models in which the relevant separation between the charged surfaces at emission was on the order of tens to hundreds of micrometres. They did not claim that the mechanism was completely settled. In fact, the measured energy concentration pushed beyond what the available models comfortably explained.

That uncertainty is useful. "Tape makes X-rays" sounds like a novelty fact. The actual experiment is about how mechanical work, charge separation, gas pressure and a rapidly changing interface can concentrate energy into individual electrons.

It is not an office hazard

The vacuum condition matters enough to keep the result in its proper setting. Contemporary reporting on the experiment noted that the researchers did not detect the same X-ray production in air. Peeling a piece of tape at your desk is therefore not equivalent to running their apparatus.

Researchers later continued studying triboelectric X-ray sources because the setup is mechanically simple and because the underlying charge-separation physics is still interesting. A 2011 Nature commentary described work on triboelectric X-ray generation as part of a broader effort to make compact X-ray sources. The tape experiment remains the memorable version because the source material is so ordinary.

There is also something pleasingly literal about the apparatus. No radioactive isotope is hidden in the chamber. The energy begins with the motor pulling tape. The strange part is what the peeling interface manages to do with it.
