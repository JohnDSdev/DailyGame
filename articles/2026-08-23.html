The moth in the Mark II was a pun

The famous insect in Harvard's 1947 computer logbook is real. The usual origin story attached to it is not.

The Smithsonian owns the page. A small brown moth is still taped into a Harvard Mark II logbook beside the handwritten line, "first actual case of bug being found." It is almost too good an artifact: a physical insect, a broken computer, and a word programmers still use every day.

The trouble starts when the story gets compressed into a sentence like "Grace Hopper coined computer bug after finding a moth in 1947." The Smithsonian's own catalog is more careful. Engineers had been calling faults "bugs" for decades, people at Harvard were already using the word for computer problems, and the logbook was probably not Hopper's. Hopper was part of the Mark II team and later helped popularize the story.

The word was older than the computer

Thomas Edison was using bug for technical trouble in the 1870s. The Thomas Edison Papers project traces a printed electrical use to 1875 and notes the word in Edison's laboratory notebooks in 1876. In a March 1878 letter to Western Union president William Orton, Edison joked about finding a bug in his telephone apparatus.

That matters because the metaphor was already established engineering slang. A bug could be a stubborn little defect hidden inside a complicated machine. No insect was required.

Peggy Kidwell, a curator at the National Museum of American History, dug into the computing history of the term for a 1998 paper in IEEE Annals of the History of Computing. Her account places "bug" at Harvard before the moth incident. The Mark I arrived there in 1944 with IBM engineers, and the lab's records from that year already talk about finding bugs.

A technical use of "bug" appears in the electrical trade journal Operator. Edison was using the same vocabulary around telegraph work.

So what happened in 1947?

On September 9, 1947, a moth was found in a relay of the Mark II. The machine was electromechanical, so a body lodged in the relay contacts could actually cause trouble. Someone on the team taped the moth into the logbook and wrote the famous note.

Read literally, the joke only works if everybody already knows the technical meaning of bug. "First actual case" is a wink: they had spent years hunting figurative bugs and had finally found an insect.

The date is also useful because it lets us separate two questions that often get mashed together. When did engineers begin calling faults bugs? Much earlier. When did one literal bug become the most famous object in the history of debugging? 1947.

Hopper and the story

Grace Hopper's connection is still genuine. She worked at Harvard on the Mark I and Mark II era machines, kept using the language of bugs, and told the moth story for years. The Smithsonian says she and the rest of the Mark II team helped spread "computer bug" and "debug." But the museum also says the surviving logbook was probably not hers.

There is a nice bit of archival irony here. The physical moth survived because the people who found it recognized a pun worth saving. That same joke later became evidence for an origin story that the joke itself contradicts.
