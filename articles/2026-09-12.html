Radio propagation

Why some AM stations almost vanish after sunset

The transmitter has not moved. The frequency has not changed. But sunset changes the path that medium-frequency radio can take through the atmosphere, and U.S. AM licensing has to account for the stations that suddenly become each other's neighbors.

There is a peculiar entry in a 2018 Federal Communications Commission order about AM broadcasting. WUMY in Memphis, then operating on 830 kHz, was cited as broadcasting with 8 kilowatts during the day and only 2 watts at night. At night it had to protect WCCO in Minneapolis, almost 700 miles away.

That nighttime power was one four-thousandth of the daytime figure. The example is unusually extreme, but the rule behind it is ordinary AM engineering. After dark, a station that was mostly a local signal can acquire a second, much longer path.

Two routes leave the same tower

An AM antenna launches energy in many directions. Some of it travels along the Earth's surface as a groundwave. Ground conductivity, frequency, antenna system and other details determine how far that useful signal goes. That path exists day and night.

Another part travels upward. In the daytime, the lower ionosphere contains a strongly absorbing D region. A National Bureau of Standards propagation handbook described its ionization as being driven mainly by solar ultraviolet radiation. Because recombination is fast at that altitude, D-region ionization, and therefore absorption, follows the Sun fairly closely. After sunset the D region's absorption becomes small.

That matters because medium-frequency energy which survives the lower ionosphere can be bent back toward Earth higher up. Radio engineers call the returned signal a skywave. The ionosphere is not a polished ceiling, so "reflection" is a convenient picture rather than a literal mirror bounce. Refraction through an ionized, changing atmosphere is closer to the physics.

The interference problem arrives with the extra range

The FCC's 2013 AM revitalization notice put the regulatory problem plainly: daytime signals travel principally by groundwave, while nighttime skywave can carry the same station hundreds of miles. If every station simply kept its daytime facilities after sunset, co-channel and adjacent-channel interference would rise over much larger areas.

So an AM license can have different daytime and nighttime operating parameters. A station may reduce power. A directional array can be phased so that radiation is suppressed toward another station. Some Class D facilities have very small authorized nighttime powers, and nighttime Class D operation does not receive the same interference protection as Class A or Class B service. The current engineering rules in 47 CFR § 73.182 spell out those protection relationships.

This is why "the station gets weaker at night" is only half the story. Your local transmitter may indeed cut power or change pattern, while distant stations on the same part of the dial are simultaneously becoming easier to hear by skywave.

On an analog receiver the transition can sound messy rather than dramatic. A familiar station may fade, another may ride underneath it, or two carriers may beat against each other as propagation changes. The ionosphere is moving too. Nighttime reception is not a fixed giant coverage map switched on at sunset.

Sunrise runs the process backward

The old Bureau of Standards handbook gives a useful clue about the timing. D-region absorption increases with the Sun's elevation because the ionization source is solar radiation and recombination there is rapid. The long path that became practical after dark is progressively attenuated again after sunrise.

This day-night cycle is old enough to be embedded in the legal architecture of American broadcasting. The machinery around it can be surprisingly specific: protected contours, directional arrays, critical hours, and licenses with separate powers. Yet the trigger is overhead every evening. Sunlight stops maintaining one low ionospheric layer, and stations hundreds of miles apart have to start behaving as neighbors.
