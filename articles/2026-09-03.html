Some bacteria grow compass needles inside themselves

A chain of iron-mineral crystals can make a bacterium behave like a tiny magnetic dipole. The strange part is how deliberately the cell builds the chain.

In 1975, microbiologist Richard Blakemore reported bacteria from marine sediments whose swimming direction followed the local magnetic field. A weak laboratory field could turn them. Inside the cells were iron-rich particles held in membrane vesicles. Blakemore suggested that the particles gave each cell a magnetic moment strong enough to explain the behavior.

The basic observation survived much better than the first vocabulary around it. The structures are now called magnetosomes. In well-studied species such as Magnetospirillum magneticum and M. gryphiswaldense, a magnetosome contains a crystal of magnetite, Fe3O4, wrapped by a membrane. Mature crystals in these model species are around 45 nanometres across. The cell arranges many magnetosomes in a line, so their magnetic moments add instead of pointing every which way.

At that scale, the chain acts like a permanent compass needle inside a living cell. The field does not tell the bacterium where food is. It biases the direction the cell points, and the bacterium still swims with flagella and responds to chemical conditions. In sediments and water columns where oxygen changes sharply with depth, magnetic alignment can reduce a three-dimensional search to something closer to travel along a preferred axis.

The crystals are organelles, not loose rust

Calling the particles "iron-rich inclusions" understates the machinery. Electron cryotomography and later genetic work showed that magnetosomes are organized membrane compartments. In M. magneticum, the magnetosome membrane is an invagination of the cell membrane. An actin-like protein called MamK helps organize the compartments into a chain.

A 2010 genetic dissection of the magnetosome island, a region of DNA containing many of the relevant genes, found that magnetosome construction can be split into steps. Different genes are needed for making the membrane compartment, sending proteins to it, and growing the magnetite crystal. Delete the wrong part of the system and the cell may still make empty compartments, malformed crystals, or no useful magnetic chain at all.

This is one reason magnetotactic bacteria are interesting beyond their odd swimming behavior. Bacteria are often introduced in school as bags of chemistry with no internal architecture worth mentioning. Magnetosomes are a clean counterexample. They are membrane-bounded bacterial organelles with dedicated assembly genes and a cytoskeletal positioning system.

A compass is useful only with the rest of the cell

"The bacteria follow north" is too simple. Blakemore's original northern-hemisphere samples tended to swim in the direction that carried them downward along the inclined geomagnetic field. Later work found south-seeking populations in the Southern Hemisphere. Even that picture has exceptions, and modern reviews treat magnetotaxis together with aerotaxis and other sensory behavior rather than as a stand-alone compass command.

The physics is simpler than the ecology. A magnetic dipole in a field experiences a torque that tends to align it with the field. Putting many single-domain magnetic crystals into an ordered chain gives the bacterium a larger, more stable magnetic moment. Once aligned, the cell still has to decide whether to swim forward, reverse, or change its behavior because of oxygen and other local conditions.

The cell controls the mineral

Magnetite is common geology. What is unusual here is biological control over where and how it precipitates. Reviews of magnetosome biogenesis describe a sequence that includes membrane formation, recruitment of magnetosome proteins, iron transport, redox control of mineral formation, crystal growth and chain assembly. Roughly thirty genes in the best-studied systems sit in or around the magnetosome gene island, although the exact genetic requirements vary among groups.

The crystals are also not all magnetite. Some magnetotactic bacteria make greigite, Fe3S4, and the organisms themselves span very different bacterial lineages and cell shapes. That diversity makes the word "bacteria" in the title do a lot of work.

Still, the small physical object at the center of the story is wonderfully literal: a bacterium can manufacture a line of magnetic crystals, mount it inside itself, and let Earth's field turn the whole cell.
