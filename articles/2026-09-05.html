Glass frogs hide most of their red blood cells while they sleep

A sleeping glass frog does something that would be alarming in most vertebrates: it pulls almost nine-tenths of its red blood cells out of circulation and packs them into its liver.

Blood is a problem if you are trying to be transparent. Red blood cells contain hemoglobin, which absorbs visible light strongly enough that a network of blood vessels remains conspicuous even when the tissue around it transmits plenty of light. The northern glass frog Hyalinobatrachium fleischmanni has translucent muscles and skin, but that alone does not erase its circulation.

Carlos Taboada, Jesse Delia and colleagues found that the frog changes the circulation instead. In a 2022 Science paper, they measured resting frogs becoming two to three times more transparent while about 89 percent of their red blood cells left the circulating blood and accumulated in the liver. When the frogs became active, the cells returned to the rest of the body.

The number is striking, but the location matters just as much. The liver is a compact place to put a large optical liability. In these frogs it is also highly reflective, which helps keep the concentrated blood from showing through as one dark red organ.

The imaging problem was unusually awkward

The obvious way to find the missing blood was also a good way to ruin the phenomenon. According to the Duke team, awake, stressed or anesthetized frogs refill their peripheral circulation and become more opaque. The researchers needed to see inside an animal that was quietly sleeping.

They used photoacoustic microscopy. A laser pulse is absorbed by molecules in tissue; that tiny burst of heating produces ultrasonic waves, and the instrument reconstructs where the absorbing material is. Red blood cells provide their own contrast because hemoglobin absorbs the light. No injected dye was needed.

That method let the team map blood inside resting frogs without the sort of intervention that would wake them or change the circulation they wanted to measure. The frogs slept upside down, as they normally do under leaves. The photoacoustic images showed a strong red-cell signal collecting in the liver while the vessels elsewhere lost it.

Transparency has an oxygen bill

Taking red blood cells out of circulation also takes away most of the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity. The 2022 study therefore describes a tradeoff that is physiological as well as optical. The frog can sit very still and become harder to see, but the same state would be a poor arrangement for sustained movement.

When activity resumes, so does ordinary-looking circulation. The liver releases red blood cells, the vessels become optically darker, and the animal has many more oxygen carriers moving through its tissues. The paper did not establish a complete control mechanism for that redistribution.

There is another unresolved problem. Packing red cells together at such high density would usually make clotting and blocked vessels an immediate concern in a vertebrate. The frogs apparently avoid that while sleeping and then reverse the sequestration when they wake. How they prevent pathological clotting is still an open question in the work.

Why moving blood changes the view

Transparency is often discussed as a property of tissue: reduce pigments, reduce scattering, match refractive indices. The glass frog adds a moving part. A major absorber is present when the animal needs it and hidden when the animal can afford to rest.

The researchers measured the optical effect with calibrated photography and modeled how much the blood contributed to attenuation. Their photoacoustic measurements supplied the anatomical piece. Those two approaches agreed on the same basic event: the circulation clears during rest because the red cells have been concentrated elsewhere.

This does not make the frog invisible. Its eyes, bones and organs remain visible, and "glass frog" covers animals with varying degrees of translucency. The result is more modest and more interesting: one vertebrate can change how transparent it is by changing where it keeps its blood.
