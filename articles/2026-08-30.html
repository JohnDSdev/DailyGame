Desert ants carry an odometer in their legs

In 2006, researchers changed the length of desert ants' legs. The ants still knew which way home was. They stopped looking for home at the wrong distance.

Cataglyphis fortis forages on salt flats in North Africa where a route can be poor in the landmarks that make navigation easy for us. An ant may wander while searching for food, then return toward its nest by a much straighter path. That requires two running estimates: direction and distance.

The direction part was already well studied. Desert ants use a skylight compass, including the sun and the polarization pattern of the sky. Distance was harder to pin down. Optic flow was an obvious candidate, but experiments had pointed elsewhere. Matthias Wittlinger, Rüdiger Wehner and Harald Wolf tested a wonderfully literal possibility: perhaps the animal measures travel through its own walking movements.

Stilts and stumps

The 2006 experiment trained ants to walk from the nest to food through a channel. At the feeder, some ants had their effective leg length increased; others had their legs shortened. The altered ants were then allowed to begin the homeward trip. The long-legged ants searched too far past the nest position. The short-legged ants began searching too soon. Unmodified controls searched around the expected distance.

That pattern is what a stride-based odometer predicts. If the outbound trip calibrated a certain amount of walking, then suddenly making each homeward stride longer causes the same accumulated walking signal to cover more ground. Shorter strides do the opposite.

The phrase "step counter" is memorable, but it can be too simple. A 2007 follow-up used high-speed video to measure what the leg manipulations actually did to stride length, stride frequency and walking speed. The authors described the mechanism as a stride integrator: the relevant signal accounts for stride length, not merely a tally that adds one for every footfall.

This matters because ants do not walk like wind-up toys with one fixed step size. Later measurements of C. fortis over a large speed range found that stride length increases with speed. At high speeds, stride frequency approaches a plateau around 30 Hz while the strides continue to lengthen. A useful odometer has to survive those changes.

The simple story gets stranger

In 2009, Kathrin Steck, Wittlinger and Wolf tried to break the stride integrator more aggressively. They disrupted the normal alternating tripod gait by removing selected legs, and they made ants cross corrugated surfaces with wavelengths around normal stride lengths. The ants stumbled; some footfall patterns became coupled to the corrugations. Yet their distance estimates and homing performance were largely unaffected.

That result does not erase the stilts-and-stumps experiment. It narrows what the mechanism can be. The odometer seems to use signals that remain useful even when the visible gait is badly disturbed. The 2009 authors argued that robust sensory signals from the legs are a likely part of the answer.

The ant is not known to keep an integer in its head called "number of steps." "Pedometer" is a useful shorthand for a biological distance gauge tied to locomotion.

There is another complication. Desert ants can deal with slopes. Earlier experiments sent them through uphill and downhill channels and then tested them on level ground. Their indicated home distance tracked the horizontal, ground-projected distance rather than simply the longer path their feet had traveled over the slopes. So the odometer sits inside a larger path-integration system that also has to account for the geometry of the route.

The oddness of the stilt experiment is what makes it useful. Changing one physical quantity, leg length, shifted one part of navigation while leaving the compass problem intact. It turned an invisible estimate inside a tiny nervous system into a mistake you could measure on the ground.
