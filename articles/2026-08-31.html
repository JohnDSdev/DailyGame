Plant physiology

Skunk cabbage keeps a warm flower in freezing air

Eastern skunk cabbage burns stored carbohydrate fast enough to regulate the temperature of its flowering structure. The effect is measurable in watts, and it has limits.

In November 1974, botanist Roger M. Knutson published two pages in Science about a plant that was behaving more like a small heater than a passive piece of vegetation. The eastern skunk cabbage, Symplocarpus foetidus, produces heat in the spadix, the knob-shaped flowering structure inside its hoodlike spathe. Knutson measured spadices 15 to 35 °C warmer than surrounding air while ambient temperatures ranged from -15 to +15 °C. He also found that this high respiratory activity could continue for at least 14 days.

The plant is not simply warming whenever it can. It regulates. When the air cools, respiration can increase; when conditions change, heat production changes with them. A later field study in Canada continuously measured temperature, oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production in flowering plants. Between ambient temperatures of 3 and 24 °C, spadix temperatures fell in a much narrower band, 16 to 26 °C. The largest heat-production rate reported in that study was about 0.26 W for a roughly 2 g spadix.

That last number is small in household terms. It is not small for two grams of flower tissue. The 1999 authors measured respiration directly and found that it rose as the air became colder. Their data also exposed a boundary that gets lost in the popular version of this story. Below about 3 °C, some inflorescences stopped maintaining the warm state and dropped toward freezing. Some froze when air temperature reached roughly -10 °C. Plants that had switched cool could return quickly to the warm state after the air rose above about 3 °C.

So "skunk cabbage melts snow" is a poor summary of the interesting part. Snow around a warm spathe can certainly disappear under suitable conditions, but the physiology is stranger than a permanently hot plant. The flower has a regulated warm state, a cool state and a transition between them. In the 1999 measurements, that switch followed the balance between metabolic heat production and heat loss to the environment.

The flower senses temperature locally

Researchers have tried to find where the temperature control lives. In one 2003 experiment, removing the spathe or a leaf did not abolish regulation. Holding the central stalk at fixed temperatures did not stop the spadix from responding to changes in ambient air either. The authors concluded that the spadix itself contains both the temperature-sensing machinery and the heat-producing tissue needed for regulation.

A year later, the same research group reported something even less intuitive. Under changing conditions, the spadix temperature could oscillate with a period of about 60 minutes. The oscillation was triggered by changes in spadix temperature smaller than 0.9 °C, and it did not require light. The paper treated those cycles as evidence of an active regulatory process instead of simple thermal inertia.

There is still room between describing the behavior and fully explaining its control. A 2007 modeling paper reproduced measured temperature responses with a negative-feedback model, but a successful model is not a molecular wiring diagram. Work on skunk cabbage and related thermogenic aroids has instead built the mechanism piece by piece around mitochondrial respiration.

Respiration with less emphasis on ATP

Ordinary cellular respiration uses an electron-transport chain to help make ATP. Thermogenic plant tissues can route more of the chemical energy toward heat. In the related Asian skunk cabbage Symplocarpus renifolius, researchers have found strong expression of alternative oxidase in thermogenic florets, along with other mitochondrial pathways associated with energy dissipation. A 2024 transcriptome and metabolome study again found high alternative-oxidase expression through the thermogenic stage and identified additional metabolic changes that track the onset of heating.

That related species is useful evidence about how this group of plants can make heat, but it should not be quietly substituted for S. foetidus. The field thermoregulation numbers above come from eastern skunk cabbage. Different papers in this literature use different Symplocarpus species, and some older classifications complicate the names further.

A useful distinction: thermogenesis means producing metabolic heat. Thermoregulation means changing heat production or loss so temperature stays within a narrower range. Skunk cabbage does both during particular flowering stages.

There is a practical cost. Heat is energy that could have gone elsewhere. The 1999 study found a respiratory quotient near 1.0, consistent with carbohydrate as the fuel, and calorimetry indicated that energy was being imported from the root. Heating also does not run through the plant's whole life. Earlier work found the strongest regulated heating during the receptive female stage of the inflorescence, with thermogenesis declining later as the flower develops.

The 1974 paper ends with a comparison that is still striking because it is quantitative rather than poetic: for at least part of flowering, oxygen consumption was comparable to that of a homeothermic animal of similar size. The flower is still a plant. For a while, though, two grams of it can spend stored carbon to hold a temperature tens of degrees above the air.
