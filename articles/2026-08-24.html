Lunar laser ranging

The Moon still answers lasers from Apollo 11

Three Apollo crews left passive reflector arrays on the lunar surface. More than half a century later, observatories still time the few photons that make the round trip.

Reading time: about 6 minutes

The hardware at Tranquility Base has no battery to die. Apollo 11 astronauts placed an array of corner-cube reflectors on the Moon in July 1969. Apollo 14 and Apollo 15 later left larger arrays, and Soviet Lunokhod rovers carried reflectors too. A corner cube sends incoming light back toward its source over a useful range of angles, so an observatory on Earth can fire a short laser pulse at one of these arrays and look for the return.

The return is not a bright flash. NASA's current open-data description says the signal collected by the telescope is typically only a few photons. The useful measurement is the elapsed time. Light makes the trip to the Moon and back; the timing becomes a distance after the observatory accounts for the atmosphere, station position, reflector position and the rest of the geometry.

About two and a half seconds of waiting

NASA gives the Moon's average orbital distance as 384,400 kilometers. Using the defined speed of light, 299,792.458 kilometers per second, a simple vacuum round trip at that distance is about 2.56 seconds. Real lunar laser ranging is more exact than this little calculation because the station and reflector are not sitting at the centers of Earth and Moon, and both bodies are moving while the pulse is in flight.

A few nanoseconds of timing error correspond to roughly a meter of light travel, so better timing, better models and better reflector geometry buy real scientific leverage. The International Laser Ranging Service says lunar ranging data are used to study the Moon's orbit and rotation, its internal structure, Earth-Moon dynamics and tests of gravitational physics.

This is still an operating experiment

"Apollo experiment" can sound like something that stopped when the astronauts came home. Lunar laser ranging did not. NASA Goddard lists the project as operational. The International Laser Ranging Service's July 2026 report card records lunar returns during the preceding 12 months from Apache Point in New Mexico, Grasse in France, Matera in Italy and Wettzell in Germany. The report includes measurements to the Apollo 11, 14 and 15 arrays and the two Soviet rover reflectors.

The old targets also have limitations. A 2024 NASA technical report notes that the Apollo-era arrays are still useful but show degradation, and their physical size and tilt now contribute measurement error as researchers push for higher precision. Modern lunar missions have started carrying smaller laser retroreflector arrays. In work spanning February 2023 through December 2024, NASA researchers even ranged from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter to reflectors on the surface, including the Apollo 11 and Apollo 14 arrays and newer targets carried by Chandrayaan-3 and Chang'e 6.

The Apollo arrays are passive. They do not prove that some forgotten instrument package is still powered on. Their trick is much simpler: geometry, glass and an observatory willing to aim very carefully.

There is a pleasing mismatch in scale here. The object on the Moon is basically an array of optical prisms. The measurement that comes back has fed decades of work on lunar motion and gravity. In July 1969, the first array was another item on an astronaut checklist. In the ILRS tables for 2026, Apollo 11 still has its own column.
