today's game: Lockwater

A hot sawtooth can propel a water drop

A Leidenfrost drop barely touches the surface beneath it. Put that same levitating drop over a row of asymmetric teeth, and the vapor escaping underneath can give the drop a preferred direction.

On a sufficiently hot surface, a liquid can sit above a thin layer of its own vapor instead of wetting the solid. Johann Gottlob Leidenfrost described the effect in 1756. The vapor layer is why drops on an overheated pan can survive surprisingly long and skate around with very little friction.

Heiner Linke and colleagues added a geometric nuisance in 2006: they machined asymmetric, ratchet-like grooves into the hot surface. Their droplets did not wander symmetrically. They moved in one preferred direction. The effect appeared for several liquids and over a range of temperatures in the film-boiling regime. The paper proposed that vapor flow in the gap was being turned into a viscous push by the sawtooth geometry.

The vapor is doing mechanical work

The useful clue came from replacing liquid with dry ice. In 2011, Guillaume Lagubeau, Marie Le Merrer, Christophe Clanet and David Quéré reported that sublimating solid pieces also self-propelled on hot ratchets. A rigid piece cannot reshape its liquid underside to crawl. It still produces gas, though. Their measurements supported a picture in which vapor escaping through the asymmetric gap is rectified by the teeth and exerts a directional thrust through viscous stress.

That mechanism was developed further in a 2011 EPL paper and a 2013 Physical Review E model. The 2013 work treated the vapor as pressure-driven flow in a thin gap and recovered the observed dependence of propulsive force on the size of the sublimating solid. In other words, the teeth are not tiny paddles striking the drop. The drop is separated from them by gas; the gas transmits the force.

The surface can steer something it barely touches because it shapes the escape route of the vapor between them.

There was still an argument about which gas physics mattered most

The broad vapor-rectification picture did not settle every detail. Alois Würger proposed a different account in Physical Review Letters in 2011, centered on thermal creep caused by the asymmetric temperature profile near each ratchet summit. That model also produces a directed gas flow. The competing descriptions operate at different levels of the same thin vapor region, and later work has continued to test which terms dominate for particular tooth sizes and operating conditions.

Scale matters. A 2011 study of micro- and sub-micrometer ratchets found much faster droplet motion as the ratchet period was reduced, reporting water-drop speeds up to about 40 cm/s in its smallest structures near the onset of motion. That is one reason a neat verbal explanation is not enough: changing the teeth changes the gas flow, and eventually the approximations used for millimeter-scale grooves stop being interchangeable.

The ordinary Leidenfrost effect is already a moving boundary problem

The ratchet experiment is easier to appreciate once the flat-surface case stops looking static. Vapor is continually generated under the drop and continually escapes. The underside is not a rigid air bearing. High-speed interference measurements have reconstructed a changing vapor gap with oscillations and distinct modes of motion.

So the sawtooth does not have to create motion from nothing. There is already a sustained vapor flow powered by heat. The asymmetric surface gives that flow a directional geometry, and the levitating body responds to the resulting stress.
