Metrology

The metre was supposed to fit the Earth

The original metric unit of length came from a survey of a meridian. Then the metre spent two centuries moving from the planet, to metal, to krypton light, and finally to a fixed speed of light.

Reading time: about 6 minutes

In March 1791, the French Academy of Sciences proposed a unit of length tied to the size of the Earth: one ten-millionth of the meridian quadrant from the equator to the pole. Louis XVI approved the plan that May. The number was chosen so that the full pole-to-equator quadrant would be 10,000 kilometres in the new system.

That left a practical problem. Nobody could put a ruler from the equator to the North Pole. Jean-Baptiste Delambre and Pierre Méchain were sent to survey a much shorter arc of the same meridian, from Dunkirk to Barcelona, beginning in 1792. Their geodetic measurements were used to infer the larger quadrant. In 1799 the result was embodied in a platinum bar deposited in the Archives of the Republic.

The 1799 archival metre was intended to realize the Earth-based definition after the Dunkirk-to-Barcelona survey.

The Earth idea did not survive as the legal standard

The Earth had been useful as a political idea because it did not belong to a particular workshop or ruler. It was less useful as something a laboratory could reproduce whenever it needed a metre. The 1799 bar therefore became the working reference. Later geodetic work also showed that the adopted metre was not an exact ten-millionth of the actual meridian quadrant. NIST's history of the National Bureau of Standards describes the original realization as erroneous by the standards of later measurements.

International metrology eventually made the artifact explicit. In 1889 the first General Conference on Weights and Measures sanctioned a platinum-iridium International Prototype Metre. A metre was the distance between two engraved lines on that bar at a specified temperature. This was easier to compare than remeasuring the planet, but the definition still lived in one physical object kept near Paris.

The metric system did not jump directly from “the Earth” to “the speed of light.” For most of its history, laboratories worked through physical or optical standards that could be compared much more precisely than the original meridian survey.

In 1960, the metre stopped being a bar

The 11th General Conference replaced the prototype with a definition based on a particular radiation from krypton-86. The BIPM says the change was made to improve the accuracy with which the metre could be realized. Interferometry let laboratories count wavelengths rather than compare their own bars against the international prototype.

That definition lasted only 23 years. Measurements of optical frequency and wavelength had improved enough that, by 1983, the old krypton definition was limiting how well the speed of light could be known in metres per second. The 17th General Conference reversed the relationship: it fixed the speed of light at exactly 299,792,458 metres per second and defined the metre through the distance light travels in vacuum during 1/299,792,458 of a second.

This is a slightly strange thing to read the first time. The speed of light is no longer something that metrologists measure and then report with a growing number of decimal places in SI units. Its numerical value in metres per second is exact because the metre is defined so that it is exact.

The wording changed again in 2019

The physical content of the 1983 definition survived the 2019 revision of the SI, but the wording was recast around defining constants. The BIPM now states that the metre is defined by taking the fixed numerical value of the speed of light in vacuum, c, to be 299,792,458 when expressed in metres per second. The second is itself defined using the caesium-133 hyperfine transition frequency.

So the modern metre is chained to two things that can be realized anywhere with sufficiently good equipment: a defined atomic frequency and a fixed value for the speed of light. The platinum-iridium prototype from 1889 still exists at the BIPM. It just has no special power over the length of your ruler anymore.

There is a nice historical loop in this. The people designing the metric system wanted a unit that was not somebody's private yardstick. Their first attempt used the size of the Earth and still ended up needing a platinum bar. Two centuries later, the unit finally became independent of any unique object, though not in the way they had imagined in 1791.
