Bumblebees can feel a flower's electric field with their hairs

A flower has color, odor and shape. It also sits in the atmospheric electric field. A flying bumblebee carries charge of its own, and the force between bee and flower can move hairs on the bee's body enough for its nervous system to notice.

In 2013, Dominic Clarke, Heather Whitney, Gregory Sutton and Daniel Robert gave Bombus terrestris a choice between artificial flowers. Some feeders carried an electric potential and some did not. The bees learned which electrical condition predicted a sugar reward, and they could also discriminate different spatial patterns of electric field. Remove the useful electrical difference and the learned preference disappeared.

The same paper measured real petunias. A visiting bee changed the electrical potential of the flower, and the change persisted after the visit. The experiment did not establish that a bee in the wild reads that change as a "recently emptied" sign. That is a plausible use for a fast-changing cue, not something the 2013 result by itself proved.

The sensor turned out to be hair

The 2013 paper left an awkward question: what part of a bee could detect an electric field in air? Fish can use conductive water as part of an electroreceptor. Air is a poor conductor, so the same arrangement does not transfer cleanly.

Sutton, Clarke, Erica Morley and Robert tested two candidates in 2016: antennae and the fine mechanosensory hairs covering a bumblebee. They used laser Doppler vibrometry to measure tiny movements under applied electric fields. Both antennae and hairs moved, but the hairs moved faster and farther. Then they recorded from the nervous system. Electric-field-driven hair motion produced a neural response; the corresponding antenna motion did not.

There is a useful bit of physics hiding in that result. The electric field does not have to enter a special electrical organ. It can exert a mechanical force on a charged hair, and an ordinary mechanosensory system can detect the bend. The 2016 experiments also found much stronger mechanical responses when the bee itself carried charge.

For the bee, an electric field can arrive as movement.

The charge is not a laboratory costume

One worry was that charged bees might be an artifact of indoor flight arenas. Clara Montgomery, K. Koh and Daniel Robert measured foraging bumblebees outdoors in 2019 with a non-contact ring sensor. The bees were almost always positively charged, and the measured charges were similar to laboratory values. Weather mattered: warm, dry conditions were associated with more positive charge.

That makes the 2016 mechanism easier to take seriously outside the lab. A charged bee approaching a flower experiences an electrostatic scene that changes with distance, plant geometry, weather and previous visitors. A 2017 review of the work estimated that modeled fields a few centimeters from a flower can be strong enough to drive the hair responses seen experimentally. The exact field around any particular flower is messier than the cartoon above.

A cue does not need to work alone

Bees already use color, odor and shape. Clarke and colleagues found that adding an electric cue could speed learning when bees had to distinguish similar colors. That is probably the more interesting way to think about the result: electroreception joins the other sensory evidence available at the flower rather than replacing it.

There is another loose end. A 2021 experiment found that electrical stimulation from bumblebee visits could increase volatile emission in Petunia integrifolia, but not in the other tested species, Antirrhinum majus. The electrical encounter may therefore affect both sides of the visit in ways that depend on the plant. Two species are not a general rule, and the authors did not claim one.
