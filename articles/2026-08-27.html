Atmospheric optics / August 27, 2026

The night sky makes its own light

Even with the Moon gone, the Sun well below the horizon, and every lamp switched off, Earth's atmosphere still glows. Astronomers have to subtract it. Astronauts can see it edge-on.

A genuinely dark terrestrial sky is not black. Part of the leftover light is starlight scattered by dust, part is unresolved stars and galaxies, and part is sunlight scattered by interplanetary dust. But the atmosphere adds light of its own. The name for that component is airglow.

The easiest airglow to recognize from orbit is a thin green band following Earth's curve. NASA describes the strongest visible feature as atomic oxygen at 557.7 nanometres. In a Space Shuttle photograph from December 11, 2000, the band sat near 100 km altitude and looked much brighter edge-on because the camera's line of sight passed through a long stretch of the emitting layer.

Three lines in the upper atmosphere

557.7 nmThe familiar green oxygen line. Nightglow observations put its main lower-thermosphere layer around 100–120 km; one 1981 sounding-rocket experiment measured the 557.7 nm emission alongside atomic oxygen between 80 and 120 km.

Why oxygen glows after sunset

Sunlight spent the day doing chemistry in the upper atmosphere. Ultraviolet radiation breaks molecules apart and leaves atoms, molecules and ions in states that can later react or relax. At night, some of that stored chemical energy comes back out as photons.

The green line has a particularly neat route. Older NASA technical literature describes the lower-thermosphere nightglow as oxygen atoms recombining through a three-body reaction, leaving an oxygen atom in an excited state. When that atom drops to a lower state, it can emit a 557.7 nm photon. A 1965 atmospheric study likewise distinguishes that nighttime recombination mechanism from the processes that dominate the daytime green line.

The chemistry is not identical at every altitude. Red atomic-oxygen airglow around 630.0 nm is associated mainly with the higher ionospheric F region, where the air is thin enough for a long-lived excited oxygen state to survive collisions long enough to radiate. Nearer the dense lower atmosphere, collisions can quench that state before it emits.

Airglow is not an aurora

They can use the same atom and even the same green wavelength, which is why photographs are easy to misread. The energy source is different. Green aurora comes from energetic charged particles arriving along Earth's magnetic field and colliding with atmospheric oxygen. NASA places common green aurora roughly between 100 and 250 km. Ordinary airglow is much more widespread and is driven largely by solar photochemistry followed by nighttime reactions.

Airglow also moves. Waves in the upper atmosphere can make bands, ripples and large changes in brightness. One equatorial observation combined an airglow imager, photometer, meteor radar and the TIMED satellite and tracked a mesospheric bore moving north at 52–58 m/s. The disturbance showed up in both hydroxyl emission near 87 km and the 557.7 nm oxygen emission near 96 km.

The practical nuisance

If you are trying to measure something faint through the atmosphere, airglow is foreground light. Its brightness varies with time, season, latitude and upper-atmosphere dynamics, so it cannot always be removed by subtracting one fixed value. That variability is annoying for astronomy and useful for atmospheric science, because the same glow records what the thin air around 90 to 300 km is doing.

From the ground, most people will never identify the oxygen line by eye. From orbit, the geometry does the favor for you: look sideways through enough glowing atmosphere and the layer draws itself as a narrow luminous rim.
