A fair coin is streakier than you think

People do tend to make fake coin flips alternate too much. The interesting part is what researchers can, and cannot, infer from that.

DAY1,048,576
POSSIBLE 20-FLIP STRINGS

Take a piece of paper and invent twenty flips of a fair coin. Use H and T. Do it before reading much farther if you want to catch your own first instinct.

Maya Bar-Hillel and Willem Wagenaar spent part of a 1991 review looking at experiments built around tasks like this: people either judged whether a sequence looked random or tried to produce one themselves. Across the earlier work, one result appeared often enough to be hard to ignore. People tended to switch between the two outcomes more frequently than a fair coin does, and they were reluctant to produce long runs.1

A coin does not try to look balanced

Twenty flips contain nineteen boundaries where the result could stay the same or switch. For an independent fair coin, each boundary has a one-half chance of being a switch, so the expected number of switches is 9.5. A person trying to make the string look suitably mixed will often push that number upward.

Runs are the other half of it. Five heads in a row at one specified position has probability 1/32. But a twenty-flip string has many possible places where a run can start, and the possibilities overlap. If you enumerate all 220 = 1,048,576 equally likely strings, 480,670 contain at least one run of five or more identical results. That is about 45.84%.

So a run like HHHHH can look more suspicious than it should when it sits inside a longer sample. In twenty flips, clumps of that size turn up fairly often.

The phrase was “local representativeness”

Bar-Hillel and Wagenaar described the bias as local representativeness. In a short stretch, people want both outcomes to show up in roughly the right proportions, while also avoiding obvious-looking patterns. A short sample is expected to carry the character of the whole process.

This is a strange requirement to impose on a coin. An independent process promises a probability on each flip. It does not promise that every window of six, ten, or twenty flips will contain a tasteful mixture of heads and tails.

There is a second wrinkle. Perfect alternation, HTHTHTHTHT, also looks wrong to most people even though it is exactly as likely as any other particular ten-flip string. We are not simply maximizing alternation. We seem to be aiming for a narrow visual idea of disorder: enough changes, few long runs, but not an obvious repeating machine.

“Humans are bad at randomness” is too broad

Raymond Nickerson revisited the literature in a 2002 Psychological Review paper and was careful about a claim that had become much stronger than the evidence. The common statement that people are simply incapable of producing or recognizing randomness, he argued, did not have the experimental support people sometimes gave it. Instructions matter. So does the particular statistic used to grade a sequence.2

There is also a basic distinction between a random process and a random-looking product. A fair coin can produce HHHHH. A person following a rigid recipe could produce a sequence that passes several statistical checks. Looking at one short string cannot tell you, by itself, how it was generated.

Wagenaar had already complained about a related problem in a 1972 survey: experiments used different instructions and different definitions of mathematical randomness, which made the results difficult to compare cleanly.3 Those differences are one reason Nickerson later urged more caution about broad conclusions.

Try the twenty flips again

If your first invented sequence had twelve or thirteen switches and no run longer than three, that would fit the old experimental pattern. It would not diagnose some general failure of human reasoning. You were asked to imitate an underspecified idea called “random,” probably without a calculator, while also trying not to look as though you were following a rule.

After seeing the 45.84% figure, you may deliberately leave uglier streaks in your next fake sequence. At that point the task has changed. You are no longer showing what randomness looked like to you before anyone supplied the statistic.
