Mechanics

A falling cat can turn without gaining angular momentum

A rigid object released upside down cannot simply decide to rotate. A cat is not rigid. That difference kept physicists busy for decades and turned a familiar animal reflex into a useful problem in mechanics.

Reading time: about 6 minutes

The Smithsonian owns a long gelatin silver print made by Étienne-Jules Marey before 1904. Its subject is exactly what the catalog says: "Cat falling and turning in air." Frame by frame, the cat changes from upside down to feet down while nothing obvious outside the cat gives it a twist.

This sounds like a small physics problem until you state the constraint. If an object has no initial angular momentum and experiences essentially no external torque, its total angular momentum stays zero. A rigid body cannot start spinning itself in midair. Yet cats can right themselves after being released from rest.

The escape is in the word rigid.

A deliberately crude two-part cat

This toy is for the conservation law, not feline anatomy. Press "run cycle." The front and rear get opposite angular momentum in each phase. Changing their moments of inertia changes how far each half turns for the same angular momentum.

Zero total does not mean zero motion everywhere

Angular momentum is additive. Different parts of a body can carry angular momenta in opposite directions while the total remains zero. If two parts receive equal and opposite angular momentum, the part with the smaller moment of inertia rotates faster. That is the same basic relation behind a spinning skater speeding up when mass moves inward.

The little animation above exaggerates that idea. In its first phase, the front is "tucked" and the rear "extended." Equal and opposite angular momenta make the front turn farther. In the second phase, the inertias swap and the internal twist reverses. The rear now makes the larger turn. With suitable geometry, both halves can finish with a changed overall orientation even though the angular momentum ledger summed to zero throughout.

A real righting cat is more complicated than this two-rotor picture. It bends its body, changes the configuration of its legs, and uses a sequence of shape changes. The toy is useful because it kills the apparent paradox without pretending to be a full anatomical model.

Marey's photographs removed an easy answer

Late nineteenth-century motion photography made the problem harder in a productive way. Marey's falling-cat sequence let researchers inspect the motion at short intervals instead of relying on what a fast fall looked like to the eye. A 2025 review in Nature Reviews Physics notes that the photographs helped rule out an explanation based on air resistance supplying the needed angular momentum.

Marey had built his scientific career around making motion measurable. The Metropolitan Museum of Art describes his chronophotographic method as repeated exposures on one plate, used to record successive stages of movement. The falling cat fit that program unusually well: the camera could preserve body shapes that passed too quickly to separate by eye.

The historical photographs show that the cat changes shape while turning. They do not, by themselves, supply a complete dynamical theory of the maneuver.

The 1969 model used a bend, not a magic torque

Thomas Kane and M. P. Scher published a mathematical model in 1969. NASA's technical-report record describes it as a two-body mechanical system intended to reproduce the backward and forward bending motions of a falling cat. Their paper treated the animal as two connected bodies and asked whether ordinary dynamics could produce the observed reorientation from internal shape changes.

It could. The model did not need the cat to borrow a spin from the handler, the floor, or the air. The important degree of freedom was the body's changing shape. Kane and Scher even imposed a no-twist condition at the connection in their simplified model, which makes the result more interesting than the common cartoon of two cylinders merely twisting against each other.

Two years later, Kane discussed self-rotation more generally in conference proceedings indexed by NASA. The same mechanics applies to humans making controlled rotations during short periods of free fall. Astronauts know the practical version: moving limbs and changing body configuration can change orientation even when there is no floor to push against.

There is a geometric version of the answer

One way to describe the falling-cat problem is to stop asking for a single hidden source of rotation. The cat traces a loop through a space of body shapes. Orientation changes as that loop is traversed. When the body returns to roughly its original shape, its orientation in ordinary space need not return to where it started.

That is why the problem later became a standard example in geometric mechanics. Asa Hopkins' Caltech notes derive the idea from the Kane-Scher model: a deformable body can change orientation while maintaining zero total angular momentum because orientation depends on the path taken through shape space; the final shape alone does not determine it.

The distinction matters. Conservation of angular momentum forbids a free rigid cat from simply spinning up. It does not freeze the orientation of a deformable cat. The conservation law survives untouched.

And the old photographs are still useful. You can look at Marey's strip and see the missing variable: the cat is never one fixed object with one fixed geometry for very long.
