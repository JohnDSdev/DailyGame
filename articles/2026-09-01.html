Paper chemistry

Old books smell because paper gives off its breakdown products

A book can release a changing mixture of volatile compounds as cellulose, lignin, sizing and other materials age. Conservators can sample that air without cutting a piece from the page.

Open a box of old books and you are sampling chemistry. Paper is mostly a network of long cellulose molecules, plus varying amounts of lignin, sizing, fillers, inks, adhesives and binding materials. Those materials do not remain chemically fixed. Oxidation and hydrolysis slowly change them, and some of the products are small enough to leave the paper and enter the air as volatile organic compounds, or VOCs.

That is why "old book smell" is not one molecule. In 2009, Matija Strlič and colleagues used headspace analysis to study volatile products from historic paper. They found that the pattern of compounds could be correlated with properties conservators care about, including lignin and rosin content, paper acidity, carbonyl content and the degree of polymerization of cellulose. Their paper explicitly identifies acetic acid and furfural among known volatile degradation products of paper, while also reporting other candidate markers.

Furfural

The 2009 paper lists furfural as a known volatile degradation product of historic paper. Later sensor work has also used furfural as a marker associated with cellulose degradation.

The useful part is not that a chemist can put a romantic name on a smell. A sample of air above the paper may carry information about the paper without requiring a destructive sample from the page. Strlič's group called the approach "degradomics": treat the mixture of emitted compounds as a chemical fingerprint of the material's condition.

There is a catch. A bound book is a complicated object. Leather, cloth, adhesives, printing inks, past storage, pollution and microbial growth can all contribute compounds. Even two papers made from similar fibers can age differently if one is acidic and the other has an alkaline reserve. A smell is evidence, but it is not a birth certificate for the book.

Why some paper ages badly

The Library of Congress gives a fairly unglamorous explanation for a lot of brittle twentieth-century paper. Mechanical wood pulping leaves lignin in the sheet and shortens fibers. Older alum-rosin sizing can also make paper acidic. Acids catalyze hydrolysis of cellulose chains, reducing their average length and eventually their mechanical strength.

Paper can make more acids as it ages. The Library reports formic, acetic, lactic and oxalic acids among products observed from aging cellulose. Those products do not necessarily escape quickly. Paper binds polar molecules, and a book is a stack of sheets pressed together with relatively little airflow.

That stacking matters. Library of Congress accelerated-aging experiments compared single sheets with stacks. The stacked paper retained more degradation products, became more acidic and degraded faster. Researchers also noticed that the stacked samples developed an odor reminiscent of old books, while single sheets in the same general aging work did not produce the same persistent smell.

A musty smell can also mean mold or damp storage. The chemistry discussed here is the ordinary degradation chemistry of paper and book materials, not a way to diagnose whether a particular book is safe to handle.

A library has its own headspace

In 2017, Cecilia Bembibre and Strlič sampled both a historic book and the air in the library at St Paul's Cathedral in London. Their analysis found many of the same major VOCs in the book and the room. That makes sense in a space filled with emitting objects, furniture and other organic materials. The smell of a historic library is partly a property of the room, not just of any one volume.

The same study tried to document historic odor as heritage rather than treating it only as contamination. That is an odd inversion of the conservation problem: the molecules can be signs of degradation, yet people may value the resulting smell as part of the experience of a historic collection.

Modern conservation labs can go further than a human nose. The Library of Congress uses techniques including thermal-desorption gas chromatography-mass spectrometry and headspace SPME-GC-MS to identify and quantify VOCs from collections and storage materials. The point is practical. Some VOCs, including acetic acid and aldehydes such as formaldehyde, can themselves harm paper, so the air around a collection is both evidence about degradation and part of the environment that determines future degradation.

Old-book odor is therefore a moving target. It depends on what the book was made from, what has happened to it, what is stored beside it and which reaction products are still trapped between the pages when you open the cover.
