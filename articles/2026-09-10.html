A Venus flytrap remembers the first touch

Its famous two-touch rule is electrical before it is mechanical. A bent trigger hair sends a signal across the leaf, calcium rises, and for a short time the trap is easier to push over the threshold for closing.
Dionaea muscipula • action potentials • calcium signaling

Touch one of the fine hairs inside an open Venus flytrap and, usually, nothing visible happens. The trap stays open. Touch again soon enough and the two lobes snap together.

The useful word there is "usually." The old classroom version of the rule says two touches within roughly half a minute. Experiments have made that picture more interesting: one mechanical event can sometimes produce enough electrical activity to close the trap, and the plant's short memory appears to be tied to a transient rise of calcium inside its cells.

The first touch leaves an electrical trace

Venus flytraps do not have nerves. Their cells can still make action potentials, rapid changes in membrane voltage produced by ion movement. This has been measured for decades. In 1961, J. R. DiPalma, R. Mohl and W. Best reported in Science that two successive stimuli were generally needed for contraction and recorded the accompanying electrical changes.

More recent work can follow the signal spatially. In a 2020 Nature Plants study, Hiraku Suda and colleagues engineered flytraps to produce GCaMP6f, a fluorescent sensor whose brightness changes with cytosolic calcium. Mechanical stimulation of a trigger hair produced a calcium increase that began in the hair and spread into the trap. A second stimulus raised calcium farther. Trap closure correlated with crossing a threshold.

The calcium signal also faded. When the researchers delayed the second stimulus by about 30 seconds, the second rise was no longer enough to cross the putative closure threshold. A following stimulus could push it over. That gives the first touch a physical aftereffect without requiring anything resembling a neuron or a brain.

The diagram above is deliberately modest. The paper measured fluorescence, not a tidy one-dimensional "memory meter," and the authors described the calcium level associated with movement as a putative threshold. What the experiment directly gives us is the timing relationship: the first stimulus leaves elevated calcium that decays, a nearby second stimulus adds to it, and a long enough delay can prevent the sum from reaching the level associated with closure.

"Two touches" is a shortcut, not a law

There is another wrinkle. A trigger hair is a mechanical sensor, so what matters is not simply the number of times an insect's body happens to contact it. The shape and speed of the deflection matter.

Jan Burri and colleagues tested trigger hairs with a force-sensing microrobotic system for a 2020 PLOS Biology paper. They found conditions in which a single mechanical stimulus could generate two action potentials and close the trap. A fast deflection above threshold commonly produced one action potential; repeat it within about 30 seconds and the second could close the trap. At intermediate angular velocities, one sustained movement could generate two electrical signals instead.

This is a useful correction because "counting touches" sounds more digital than the machinery actually is. The plant responds to electrical events generated by a deforming sensor. In ordinary prey capture, separate contacts often provide those events. The circuitry does not need to know what a "touch" is.

The hairs themselves are surprisingly sensitive. In 2019, Sönke Scherzer and colleagues reported action potentials after controlled deflections greater than 2.9 degrees, angular velocities greater than 3.4 degrees per second, and forces above 29 micronewtons in their setup. Temperature and the recent stimulation history of a hair affected its responsiveness too.

After the trap closes, the count keeps mattering

Closure is only the beginning of the hunting cycle. In 2016, Jennifer Böhm and colleagues mechanically stimulated traps while measuring electrical signals, hormone responses and gene expression. Their Current Biology paper found that the jasmonate signaling pathway was activated after the second stimulus, while more than three action potentials were needed before genes for prey-degrading hydrolases were induced. The amount of expression rose with additional stimulation.

The same study followed a sodium transporter called DmHKT1. Its transcript abundance depended on the number of mechano-electric stimulations, and the researchers showed sodium uptake through glands of the capture organ. In one recorded cricket capture shown in the paper, the prey produced 51 action potentials during the first hour and more than 100 over six hours.

So the early electrical events do two jobs on different timescales. A short-lived signal helps decide whether the trap should close. Continued activity from struggling prey changes the chemistry of the sealed trap and the machinery used to digest and absorb the meal.

A molecular piece of the trigger

Work has also moved closer to the membrane proteins that start the signal. A 2025 Nature Communications study identified the mechanosensitive channel MSL10 as a high-sensitivity component of the flytrap's tactile system. Strong stimulation produced calcium increases in the hair and leaf blade; weaker stimulation could produce a local calcium rise at the base of the hair without propagation across the blade.

That result fits a picture in which the trigger hair is not a simple switch. Mechanical strain is converted into ion flow locally, electrical and calcium signals spread if the stimulus is sufficient, and recent activity changes what the next stimulus can do. The famous snapping leaf is the fast part you can see.
