Soil chemistry / August 28, 2026

Rain can launch soil into the air

The smell after a dry spell is partly chemistry and partly fluid mechanics. A raindrop can trap air against porous ground, burst tiny bubbles, and throw microscopic droplets back upward carrying material from the soil.

In 2015, Young Soo Joung and Cullen Buie filmed water drops hitting soil with a high-speed camera. The sequence is stranger than a splash. The drop spreads over the porous surface. Air caught underneath forms bubbles inside the drop. Those bubbles rise through a layer of water that exists for only a moment, break at the surface, and fire tiny jets upward.

The jets break into aerosol droplets. Some were only tens of micrometres across. Under one laboratory condition, the researchers recorded hundreds of jets after a single impact, with the burst happening over roughly 20 microseconds.

This did not happen for every kind of rain or every kind of ground. In their experiments, clay and sandy-clay soils produced aerosols under conditions corresponding to light and moderate rain. Sand did not. Very high impact speeds produced ordinary radial splashing instead of the bubble mechanism they were studying.

The smell has more than one source

The word petrichor is older than the aerosol video by half a century. CSIRO researchers Isabel Joy Bear and Richard Thomas used it in a 1964 Nature paper about the characteristic odour released when dry soils and rocks are moistened. Their term was deliberately broad. They were naming an odour from mineral surfaces, not declaring that one molecule was responsible for every version of the smell.

One molecule in the story is geosmin. It has the earthy smell familiar from damp soil and, less pleasantly, from musty water. In 2007, Jiaoyang Jiang, Xiaofei He and David Cane identified how Streptomyces coelicolor makes geosmin: one bifunctional enzyme converts farnesyl diphosphate through an intermediate and then into geosmin.

So the rain is doing transport as well as chemistry. Odorous material already present on or in the ground can be lifted into air in microscopic droplets. Joung and Buie tested this directly by loading a porous surface with fluorescent dye and detecting the dye after drop impacts on collection slides above the surface.

Why would a soil bacterium smell earthy?

That question got a useful answer in 2020. Paul Becher and colleagues studied Streptomyces, geosmin, and springtails, tiny soil arthropods. Springtail antennae responded to geosmin and another bacterial volatile, 2-methylisoborneol. In behavioral tests, the animals were attracted to the compounds and to sporulating bacterial colonies.

The springtails also carried bacterial spores on their bodies and spread them. The paper describes an interaction that appears beneficial to both sides: the springtails find food, while the bacteria gain transport for spores. Geosmin is therefore not merely a smell that happens to reach us after rain. At least in this system, another animal is using it as information.

Humans are eavesdropping on the same molecule, though our rainy-day smell is a mixture with a messy origin. That is probably why attempts to reduce petrichor to "the smell of geosmin" feel tidy but miss part of the phenomenon.

A raindrop is a small sampling device

A follow-up study in 2017 pushed the mechanics toward microbiology. Researchers showed that raindrop impacts could generate bioaerosols from soil, including droplets carrying bacteria. The exact environmental importance depends on what happens after launch: particles settle, collide with later raindrops, dry out, or move with wind. The 2015 paper was careful about that gap and did not claim that every aerosol it produced would travel far.

But the first few millimetres are real. A drop lands. Air is trapped. A bubble bursts. Something that was in the soil is briefly airborne.
